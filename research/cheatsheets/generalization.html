Generalization & regularization

What generalization means

Generalization is performance on new examples drawn from the same process as your data — not memorizing the training set. You optimize a training loss (loss functions); you judge success on held-out data. When train looks good and validation does not, you have a generalization gap.

Bias, variance & irreducible error
Plain language
  • Bias — systematic error from a model that is too simple or wrong inductive assumptions (underfitting). High train and val error.
  • Variance — sensitivity to which training samples you got; fits noise (overfitting). Train error low, val error high.
  • Irreducible (Bayes) error — noise in labels or truly random outcomes; no model removes it.

For squared loss at a fixed input, a classic decomposition is expected test error ≈ bias² + variance + irreducible noise. The exact algebra assumes an ensemble of models trained on different samples; the intuition survives for single deep nets: complexity trades bias against variance.

Underfit vs overfit
Signal Likely issue First levers
Train & val both poor High bias (underfit) Bigger model, better features, train longer, lower regularization
Train good, val poor High variance (overfit) More data, stronger regularization, early stop, simpler model
Val better than train Often leakage, bug, or small val set — investigate before celebrating Audit splits, augmentation on train only, fix pipeline
Learning curves & splits
Train / validation / test

Train — fit parameters. Validation — choose hyperparameters, early stopping, model selection (many peeks → val optimistically biased; keep a untouched test for final report). Same metric on train and val; plot both vs epoch or training steps.

Reading curves
  • Gap widening → overfitting; consider regularization, data, or stop earlier.
  • Both flat and high → underfitting; capacity or optimization (LR, train longer).
  • Val improves then degrades → classic early-stop point at val minimum.
  • More training data shrinks variance — curve shows val error dropping as $N$ grows.
Capacity vs data

Capacity is the model’s ability to fit complicated functions (depth, width, rank of adapters). More capacity lowers bias but raises variance when data is limited. Rule of thumb: match capacity to dataset size and task difficulty — a huge net on 500 labels will memorize unless regularized.

LLMs: pretrain capacity is fixed; bias–variance shows up most in fine-tuning, small heads, and embedding adapters (PEFT, representations), not in picking layer count for GPT-class pretrain.

Regularization
Weight penalties

Add a term that punishes large weights so the fit stays smooth and less twitchy to individual samples.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} + \lambda \sum_i w_i^2 \quad \text{(L2 / weight decay)} $$
  • L2 (weight decay) — shrinks weights toward zero; default in many transformers (AdamW decouples decay from adaptive steps).
  • L1 — sparsity; some weights driven exactly to zero.
  • $\lambda$ too large → underfit; too small → overfit.
Dropout & stochastic depth

Dropout randomly zeros activations during training; at inference use full net (often scaled). Acts like averaging many thinned networks — reduces co-adaptation and variance. Common in older CNNs/Transformers; many LLMs rely more on scale + weight decay.

Early stopping

Monitor validation metric each epoch; stop when it has not improved for patience steps; restore weights from the best val checkpoint. Cheap, strong regularizer — equivalent to limiting effective training time / capacity.

Other levers (pointers)
  • Label smoothing — softens one-hot targets; calibrates and reduces overconfidence (loss sheet).
  • Data augmentation — artificial diversity; lowers variance (domain-specific).
  • Batch size / noise — small batches add gradient noise (sometimes regularizing); not a substitute for val-based decisions.
  • Ensembling — average models; variance down, cost up.
Double descent (footnote)

In some overparameterized regimes, test error can fall again after the classical U-shaped region as capacity grows further (double descent). Modern deep nets often operate past the interpolation threshold. Still use validation curves and regularization in practice — the simple bias–variance story is the operational guide; double descent explains why “ huge model + early stop + decay” can still generalize.

Not the same as …
  • Failure modes (sheet) — gradient explosion, collapse, Adam instability during training, not val/train gap semantics.
  • Fairness “bias” — social skew in data or outputs; see agents trust & policy.
  • Statistical bias in estimators — e.g. logging only accepted outcomes; evaluation design problem.
Chooser
  • Train & val both bad → capacity, features, LR, train longer; reduce $\lambda$.
  • Train good, val bad → more data, augmentation, dropout/weight decay, early stop; shrink model or rank (PEFT).
  • NaN, loss spikes, flat loss with weird grads → failure modes first; generalization second.
  • Small fine-tune set → low rank, few epochs, early stop, strong decay; watch embed overfit on representations.
