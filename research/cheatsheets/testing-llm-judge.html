Testing — LLM as a judge

What an LLM judge is

A large language model (LLM) judge is a model prompted with a rubric to score or compare another system's outputs. It replaces human review where answers are open-ended and no single reference string exists. It is a measurement instrument, so it needs the same validation as any other: check it against human labels before trusting it.

  • Pointwise: score one answer against a rubric (1–5, or pass/fail per criterion).
  • Pairwise: pick the better of two answers (A/B). More stable than absolute scores, but costs two calls to remove order effects.
  • Reference-guided: the judge also sees a gold answer or the retrieved context, and checks the answer against it.
(a) When to use a judge
Good fit Poor fit, and what to use instead
Open-ended answers with no single correct string (summaries, support replies, explanations) Exact-answer tasks (extraction, classification, code) — use exact match, unit tests, or execution
Regression tests when a prompt, model, or retriever changes, run on hundreds of cases Facts the judge cannot check without a source — give it the context or a reference, or use a verifier tool
Properties a rubric can state: groundedness against supplied context, instruction following, tone, policy compliance Narrow domain expertise (clinical, legal) where the judge is less reliable than the model being judged — use expert review
Triage of production traffic: score a sample and send low scorers to humans Final decisions on safety-critical actions — use deterministic rules and human approval
Ranking candidate prompts or models (pairwise) The only signal you optimize against — the system learns the judge's blind spots (Goodhart's law)

Use a judge from a different model family than the system under test, and keep a human-reviewed sample alongside it.

(b) Biases and failure modes
Bias What happens Quick test
Verbosity (length) Longer answers score higher regardless of content Correlate score with token count; compare against the same correlation in human scores; pad an answer with filler and re-score
Position In pairwise mode the judge prefers the first (or second) slot Swap A and B; the preference should follow the answer, not the slot
Self-preference A judge favors outputs from its own model family or style Score the same answers with judges from two different families and compare
Style and confidence Fluent, confident, well-formatted answers beat correct but plain ones Insert a fluent factual error into a good answer; the score should drop
Leniency and compression Most scores land in a narrow band (such as 4–5), so small prompt changes look like gains Plot the score histogram; check whether known-bad answers get low scores
Unverifiable claims Without a source, the judge accepts plausible wrong facts Seed answers with false but plausible details and measure how often they pass
Prompt sensitivity and noise Rewording the rubric or sampling temperature changes scores Run the same items several times and with paraphrased rubrics; measure the spread
Rubric ambiguity “Helpful” or “good” means different things per run, so the judge falls back on length and tone Check whether two humans agree on the rubric first
(c) How to evaluate a judge

Build a calibration set. Collect 100–300 items labeled by humans with the same rubric and scale the judge uses. Include hard cases: pairs that differ in length, near-ties, and answers that are fluent but wrong. Hold part of it out so that tuning the judge does not overfit it.

Pick the agreement metric by score type.

Judge output Agreement metric
Pairwise preference (A or B) Agreement rate and Cohen's kappa
Ordinal score (1–5) Weighted kappa (quadratic weights), plus Spearman or Kendall rank correlation
Binary pass/fail Cohen's kappa, and the judge's precision and recall against the human label

Cohen's kappa corrects raw agreement for the agreement expected by chance:

kappa = (p_o − p_e) ÷ (1 − p_e)
  • p_oobserved agreement between judge and human
  • p_eagreement expected if both labeled at random with their own label frequencies

Example: the judge and the human agree on 85% of pairs and chance agreement is 50%, so kappa = (0.85 − 0.50) ÷ (1 − 0.50) = 0.70. Kappa near 0.6–0.8 is usually considered substantial; the bar depends on how much a wrong verdict costs.

Then check the following.

  • Human ceiling: measure agreement between two humans on the same items. The judge cannot reasonably be expected to exceed it.
  • Agreement where it matters: compute agreement on the items where the two versions under test differ. High overall agreement can hide failures on exactly those items.
  • Per segment: by question type, answer length, and difficulty.
  • Bias probes: run the quick tests in the previous table and record the results.
  • Uncertainty: bootstrap a confidence interval for agreement and for score differences between versions; a small gain on a few hundred items is often noise.
  • Judge change control: re-score the calibration set whenever the judge model, rubric, or sampling settings change, and require a minimum agreement before using it.
(d) How to harden or tune a judge

Work through these in order; each is cheaper than the next.

  1. Sharpen the rubric. Separate criteria (correctness, groundedness, completeness, conciseness), define each score level, and state that length is not quality and that unnecessary length is penalized.
  2. Score per criterion before the overall score. Ask for a short reason and the criterion scores, then the verdict. This reduces the halo effect of one strong impression.
  3. Give the judge evidence. Supply the retrieved context or a reference answer so it checks claims rather than guessing.
  4. Add few-shot examples from the human-labeled set, including cases where the longer or more fluent answer is worse.
  5. Control order and noise. In pairwise mode, run both orders and keep the verdict only when they agree, otherwise call it a tie. Use temperature 0, or average several samples.
  6. Control length. Compare at matched length, regress score on length and use the residual, or penalize length beyond a target explicitly.
  7. Use a different model family from the system under test, and consider an ensemble of two judges that must agree.
  8. Fine-tune only if agreement is still low and you have enough labeled data. Supervised fine-tuning on human labels, or a trained reward model, is the usual route. Direct preference optimization (DPO) trains a generator on preference pairs and is a less direct fit for a scoring model; a small set of spot checks also risks overfitting.

In production use, let the judge triage and sample, keep a rolling human-reviewed slice to detect drift in the judge itself, and ship a change only when it wins on both the judge and the human sample.

Checklist
  • Rubric written, with criteria and score levels defined.
  • Human-labeled calibration set with hard cases, and a held-out part.
  • Agreement measured with the right metric, against the human–human ceiling.
  • Length, position, and self-preference probes run and recorded.
  • Confidence intervals reported for version-to-version differences.
  • Judge model and rubric versioned; calibration re-run on any change.
  • Humans still review a sample of production outputs.
